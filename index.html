How it works

  1. Pick layers. Send each layer to the macro step (climate, which changes slowly across the state) or the micro step (soil, which changes from field to field), or leave it off. With no micro layers, the result is one pooled clustering of the macro layers.
  2. Standardize. Every layer is converted to z-scores over the whole state (subtract the Kansas mean, divide by the standard deviation), so each layer counts equally whatever its units.
  3. Macrozones. The macro layers are clustered into the chosen number of macrozones.
  4. Microzones. Inside each macrozone, the micro layers are clustered again. Every microzone therefore sits inside one macrozone, and changing the soil layers never moves a macrozone boundary.
  5. Clean small patches (optional). A majority filter replaces each pixel with the most common zone in a 3 × 3 or 5 × 5 km window, which removes isolated pixels.
  6. Crop mask (optional). Keeps only the counties where a crop is grown, without changing the zones: they are still made from the whole state, so a zone keeps the same meaning whatever crop is chosen.

Crop mask

Crop areas come from the USDA NASS Cropland Data Layer (CDL), summed by county and averaged over . Counties are ranked by their crop area, and the largest are kept until together they hold the chosen share of the Kansas area of that crop. For example, winter wheat, 90% keeps the fewest counties that together grow 90% of the state's wheat. The other counties are greyed out on the map and left out of the zone table and the downloads.

Double crops count toward both crops (for example, wheat followed by soybeans counts as wheat and as soybeans). Rangeland is the CDL Grass/Pasture and Shrubland classes, which also include hay meadows, pastures and Conservation Reserve Program grass. The crop % column of the zone table spreads each county's crop area evenly over the county, so it is a guide to how much of the crop each zone holds, not a field-level measure.

Method

k-means puts each pixel in the zone with the nearest centre and moves the centres until they settle (k-means++ starts, best of three).

How many zones

The silhouette score compares, for each pixel, the distance to its own zone with the distance to the nearest other zone. It runs from −1 to 1; higher means better-separated zones, and values above about 0.5 indicate clear structure. Scores are computed for each number of zones on a sample of 20,000 pixels (scored on 3,000 of them); auto picks the highest. Kansas climate is a smooth gradient, so the macro scores are often nearly flat. When they are, the number of zones is a choice about how much detail is useful, not something the data decide.

Correlated layers

Layers that are correlated (see Correlations) give extra weight to the gradient they share when they are in the same step. That is reasonable when each one stands for a different process, such as water supply (rainfall) and water demand (ETo).

Numbers and colours

Macrozones are numbered by the mean of the first macro layer in the list, from low to high (with rainfall first, from dry to wet). Microzones are numbered the same way by the first micro layer. Each macrozone has its own hue; its microzones are lighter and darker shades of it. Zone codes are macrozone.microzone, for example 2.1.

Downloads

The CSV lists every zone with its area and the mean of every layer, plus the settings used. The zone-map GeoTIFF is on the 1-km grid (EPSG:4326), with value = macrozone × 10 + microzone and 0 outside Kansas. With a crop mask, both cover only the kept counties (0 elsewhere in the GeoTIFF), and the CSV adds the crop share of each zone and lists the kept counties.

About

The page groups Kansas into environments (agro-ecological zones) from climate and soil layers, for uses such as choosing trial and breeding sites, scaling point crop-model runs, or placing monitoring stations. Everything runs in your browser on a 1-km grid (273,579 pixels); nothing is uploaded. The water-balance layers (deficit-days and surplus-days) come from the Kansas 1-km soil moisture model.
